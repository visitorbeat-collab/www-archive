# Working Note

I keep coming back to the same problem.

If these records really are assessments, what would make one complete?

Intelligence doesn't seem to be enough. Neither does capability, technical success, or even correcting a failure once the consequences are obvious. The observations keep going after all of those things happen.

That makes me think the standard may have less to do with achievement than I originally assumed.

Lately I've been wondering whether it has more to do with predictability.

I don't mean prediction in the technical sense. I mean whether a population can be expected to regulate its own influence when the conditions are difficult, inconvenient, or ambiguous.

What happens when the consequences are delayed? What happens when uncertainty remains and restraint is expensive? What happens when the affected system can't object, or when the benefit is immediate but the damage belongs to someone else?

More importantly, what happens when the damage belongs to the future?

A population that only behaves carefully while consequences are visible is hard to model. So is one that revises only after catastrophe, or one that understands its dependencies but stops maintaining them when doing so becomes inconvenient.

Maybe that's closer to the point.

Not whether we're intelligent. I'm increasingly convinced that question is too simple.

I don't think the issue is whether we're moral either, whatever that would even mean from their perspective. And it obviously can't be whether we make mistakes, because every document I've seen assumes that mistakes will happen.

The question may be something closer to this:

**Can increasing influence be trusted to remain coupled to self-regulation?**

I don't know whether "trust" is the right word.

It's probably too human, and maybe too interpersonal. Trust suggests intention, relationship, expectation. I'm using it because I don't have a cleaner term for what I think I'm seeing.

The uglier version would be something like:

**reliable behavioral constraint under expanding capability**

Which sounds terrible, but may actually be closer.

The reason I keep thinking about this in relation to contact is fairly obvious.

If a more advanced civilization had access to knowledge or technology that could dramatically increase what we are capable of doing, the main danger wouldn't be that we were too unintelligent to understand it.

The danger would be increasing the influence of a population whose internal limits did not increase with it.

That would also explain why the observation seems to take place over such long periods. You couldn't learn much about this from one encounter, or even from one crisis. You would have to watch what happened afterward, then later, and then after the original event was no longer culturally immediate.

You'd have to see whether a change in behavior survived changing incentives.

Whether restraint survived opportunity.

Whether a lesson remained a lesson once the consequences were no longer directly visible.

And eventually, I suppose, whether systems outside immediate human concern started to matter simply because our actions could affect them.

That takes time.

Probably a great deal of it.

I've also been thinking about the way I keep framing contact as something we might "deserve." I like that wording less every time I use it.

It turns the whole thing into a judgment of worth, and I'm not sure the material supports that.

Maybe the question isn't whether we deserve contact.

Maybe it's whether contact would make the system less stable.

If that's closer to the actual concern, then what is being measured may not be worthiness at all.

It may be trustworthiness.

I'm leaving that word provisional.

I still don't know exactly what would count as evidence for or against it, but at the moment it explains more of the structure than "intelligence" does.
