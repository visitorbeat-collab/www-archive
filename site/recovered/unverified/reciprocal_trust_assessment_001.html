1.0Assessment Basis

Transition between technological populations creates reciprocal consequence.

Assessment therefore cannot be limited to:

Can TP-3 be trusted by an external population?

A second question is required:

Can TP-3 evaluate whether an external population should be trusted?

These questions are related.

They are not equivalent.

2.0Reciprocal Trust

reciprocal trust

Mutual predictive confidence that each population will remain behaviorally constrained under conditions of shared consequence.

Reciprocal trust does not require:

identical values
identical interests
identical technology
political alignment
cultural similarity
absence of conflict

It requires sufficient confidence in behavioral limits.

3.0Capability Asymmetry

Where capability differs substantially between populations, trust conditions become unequal.

The less capable population faces greater vulnerability to:

coercion
dependency
information manipulation
inaccessible surveillance
unilateral intervention
technological domination
imposed system change

The more capable population faces different risks, including:

unpredictable diffusion
asymmetric retaliation
uncontrolled technology acquisition
rapid behavioral destabilization
conflict propagation

Asymmetry changes risk.

It does not eliminate reciprocity.

4.0Submission Error

A less capable population may interpret technological superiority as justification for obedience.

This produces:

submission error

Capability establishes:

capacity

It does not independently establish:

benevolence
accuracy
legitimacy
right to command

5.0Hostility Error

The inverse error also occurs.

Extreme capability asymmetry may be interpreted as evidence of inevitable threat.

This may produce:

defensive escalation
preemptive aggression
refusal of communication
concealment
technological acceleration
institutional panic

Capability does not independently establish hostile intent.

6.0Evaluation Under Asymmetry

A developing population must retain the ability to evaluate an external population using evidence other than capability.

Relevant variables include:

consistency between stated and observed behavior
treatment of weaker populations
respect for established boundaries
willingness to tolerate disagreement
response to refusal
transparency of consequence
correction after error
restraint where coercion would be easier

These variables may remain difficult to observe.

7.0Power / Trust Distinction

Greater power increases the consequence of misplaced trust.

Therefore:

capability asymmetry
should increase
evidentiary requirement

not:

automatic deference

8.0External Restraint

Where one population possesses overwhelming capability, restraint becomes especially informative.

Relevant behavior includes:

not exercising available control
preserving autonomous decision space
limiting unnecessary information exposure
permitting disagreement
avoiding dependence creation
maintaining reversibility
using minimum sufficient intervention

Restraint under capability asymmetry provides information concerning external reliability.

9.0Non-Intervention as Evidence

Failure to intervene does not automatically indicate restraint.

Possible explanations include:

indifference
inability
lack of awareness
strategic delay
observational preference
consequence uncertainty

Behavior must therefore be interpreted relationally.

Absence of action is not self-explanatory.

10.0Intervention as Evidence

Intervention does not automatically indicate benevolence.

Possible purposes include:

harm reduction
self-protection
experimentation
information acquisition
developmental guidance
strategic influence
unknown objectives

Observed benefit does not uniquely identify motive.

11.0Boundary Recognition

Trust increases where populations demonstrate reliable recognition of boundaries.

Relevant boundaries include:

physical
informational
biological
cultural
environmental
political
cognitive

Boundary recognition may be complicated where the populations do not classify autonomy in equivalent ways.

12.0Consent Problem

Full symmetric consent may be impossible during extreme capability asymmetry.

However, inability to obtain perfect symmetry does not eliminate the significance of:

minimizing coercion
limiting unnecessary exposure
preserving exit where possible
avoiding irreversible alteration
reducing imposed dependency

Assessment should distinguish unavoidable asymmetry from unnecessary domination.

13.0Error Correction

Trustworthiness requires observable response to error.

Relevant questions include:

Is harm acknowledged?
Is behavior modified?
Are failed assumptions retained?
Is affected-party information incorporated?
Does correction persist?

An external population incapable of revision would remain high risk regardless of capability.

14.0Mutual Uncertainty

Neither population possesses complete models of the other.

Therefore reciprocal trust must develop under uncertainty.

Premature certainty produces risk in both directions.

Useful states include:

provisional confidence
limited interaction
bounded exposure
reversible cooperation
staged access
continued independent assessment

15.0Gradual Reciprocity

Reciprocal trust may develop incrementally.

Possible progression:

observation
→ limited interaction
→ behavioral comparison
→ bounded cooperation
→ expanded access
→ deeper integration

Each stage creates additional evidence.

Each stage also creates additional consequence.

16.0Trust Without Dependence

Stable reciprocal trust should not require one population to surrender independent evaluation.

A population may trust another while retaining:

skepticism
verification
refusal capacity
competing models
internal decision processes
independent technological development

Trust and autonomy are compatible.

Dependency and trust are not equivalent.

17.0Trust Without Innocence

Trust does not require belief that another population is incapable of harm.

Any sufficiently capable population can produce harm.

Relevant question is whether harmful capability remains constrained by:

internal regulation
predictable boundary recognition
consequence modeling
reciprocal expectation
durable restraint

Trust concerns expected behavior, not assumed purity.

18.0Wider-System Condition

Once multiple technological populations interact, consequence is no longer bounded to bilateral relationships.

Actions may affect:

other populations
shared environments
transit systems
information networks
developing biospheres
populations not participating in the decision

Reciprocal trust therefore requires recognition of third-party consequence.

19.0TP-3 Relevance

TP-3 presently demonstrates mixed capacity for trust under asymmetry.

Observed patterns include:

deference to perceived expertise
suspicion of opaque authority
authority capture
coalition formation
institutional verification
conspiracy formation
rapid attribution of motive
trust based upon identity rather than behavior
revision following demonstrated failure

These patterns vary substantially across contexts.

20.0Contact Readiness Variable

A population approaching wider contact should demonstrate the ability to avoid both:

submission to superior capability
and
automatic hostility toward superior capability

while continuing evidence-based evaluation.

This condition is not presently stable within TP-3.

21.0Assessment Principle

A population is not prepared for wider community merely when others can trust it.

It must also remain capable of deciding whom it should trust.
